"Good morning. Our project is a Big Data analysis of global
earthquakes and their association with volcanic activity.

We used two official sources: the USGS earthquake catalogue and
the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program.

The pipeline downloads the data, stores the raw files in Hadoop
HDFS, cleans the data with PySpark, saves it as Parquet, and then
runs four analyses. The dashboard reads the saved results, so it
does not re-run Spark while we are presenting."

------------------------------------------------------------
 PAGE 3 - HOTSPOT ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------

[Click "Hotspot Analysis".]

"This is one of our main contributions.

A raw earthquake map can be misleading. A region with more
monitoring stations will detect more small earthquakes, so it can
look more active even if the actual activity is similar.

To handle this, we estimate a value called Mc - the magnitude of
completeness. Mc is the smallest magnitude above which we believe
the earthquake records are complete enough to compare.

We estimate Mc for every 5-degree region and every time window.
Then we count only the earthquakes above Mc.

[Point to the map.]

"The left map shows raw activity. The right map shows the corrected
activity above Mc.

The median Mc is 4.55, and the median b-value is 0.99. The b-value
comes from the Gutenberg-Richter relationship and describes the
balance between small and large earthquakes.

The main result is this: out of 198 comparable regions, 44 regions
showed an apparent increase in raw counts, but that increase
disappeared after correction.

This means the apparent growth was likely caused by better
detection, not necessarily by a real change in earthquake activity."

TRANSITION:
"Next, I will show how we group earthquakes in space and time."


------------------------------------------------------------
 PAGE 4 - SPACE-TIME CLUSTERING
------------------------------------------------------------

[Click "Space Time Clustering".]

"This page shows our space-time clustering analysis.

Normal DBSCAN groups points using only distance. Our ST-DBSCAN uses
both space and time.

Two earthquakes are neighbours only if they are within 50 km and
within 7 days of each other. A group needs at least 10 points.

[Point to the pipeline diagram.]

"Spark MLlib does not provide DBSCAN, so we implemented it ourselves.

The globe is divided into small spatial cells. ST-DBSCAN runs
inside each Spark partition. Because a cluster can cross a cell
border, we add overlapping halo events. After local clustering, we
use Union-Find to merge clusters that share the same earthquake.

[Point to the numbers.]

"On the tested sample, we found 494 clusters and 98,583 noise
points. Noise points are earthquakes that do not belong to a dense
group.

The ARI is 0.999991. ARI compares our distributed result with a
single-machine result. A value close to 1 means the two results are
almost identical.

The distributed run took about 154 seconds, while the single-machine
version took about 25 seconds. On this small dataset, Spark is slower
because of its startup and scheduling overhead. We report that
honestly."

TRANSITION:
"Now I will explain the volcano analysis."


------------------------------------------------------------
 PAGE 5 - VOLCANO ANALYSIS
------------------------------------------------------------

[Click "Volcano Analysis".]

"This page studies whether earthquake activity changes near a
volcano around the time of an eruption.

We analysed 941 confirmed eruptions at 221 volcanoes.

For each volcano, we use its own previous five years as a normal
baseline. Then we compare two windows:

- 90 days before the eruption
- 180 days after the eruption

[Point to the selected volcano and map.]

"The map shows the volcano and nearby earthquakes.

The table shows the baseline rate, pre-eruption rate, post-eruption
rate, rate ratio, confidence interval and p-values.

At a radius of 100 km, the median pre-eruption rate ratio is about
1.18. This means earthquake activity was about 18 percent above the
volcano's normal baseline before the eruption.

The post-eruption ratio is about 1.02, so it is close to the
baseline.

[Point to the radius comparison.]

"We repeat the analysis at 50, 100 and 200 kilometres to check
whether the result depends on the chosen radius.

This is an association analysis. It does not predict eruptions and
it does not prove that eruptions cause the earthquakes."

TRANSITION:
"Finally, I will show the performance comparison."


------------------------------------------------------------
 PAGE 6 - PERFORMANCE BENCHMARKING
------------------------------------------------------------

[Click "Performance Benchmarking".]

"This page compares Pandas and Spark using the same data and the
same operations.

Pandas runs on a single machine. Spark runs in local mode on this
machine, using multiple threads.

We tested data sizes from 10 percent to 100 percent, different
Spark partition counts, CSV and Parquet formats, and cached and
uncached runs.

[Point to the speed-up chart.]

"On this 681,902-row dataset, Pandas was faster for every measured
operation.

For example, group-by took about 0.034 seconds in Pandas and 0.195
seconds in Spark. The spatial join took about 1.14 seconds in Pandas
and 8.04 seconds in Spark.

Spark startup alone took about 5 seconds.

This is not a failure of Spark. It shows that Spark has high fixed
overhead, and at this data size a single machine is faster. Spark
becomes useful when the data is much larger or when we have a real
multi-node cluster.

We report this negative result honestly instead of claiming that
Spark is always faster."

We built a complete pipeline from official data sources to HDFS,
PySpark processing, Parquet storage, four analytics modules and an
interactive dashboard.

We corrected hotspot rankings for detection completeness, implemented

tested volcano-earthquake associations against volcano-specific

All results are saved and traceable to files. The main limitations
